Radiation cannot be seen, heard or smelled. Only an instrument tells you it is there. While the dose is small the body does not notice it, and once it is large it is too late to fix.

## Where it comes from

A running pile is a strong source of gamma rays and neutrons: each fission gives about 13 MeV of gamma radiation and 2.4 neutrons. After shutdown the chain reaction stops, but the fission products keep decaying. Their gamma radiation lasts for hours and days, long after the rods are in. A criticality excursion in a vessel of enriched uranium lasts an instant, but in that instant the dose one metre away is tenths of a sievert or more.

## Distance, time, shielding

Dose rate from a point source falls with the square of distance: step twice as far away and you get a quarter of the dose. Accumulated dose is dose rate times time, so work fast.

Shielding cuts radiation exponentially. For gamma rays heavy materials work best: lead halves cobalt-60 gamma every centimetre, concrete every five or six, water every fifteen. Some scattered photons still get through, which the buildup factor accounts for: behind thick shielding the real dose is several times what a narrow beam would give. Lead does almost nothing against neutrons. Hydrogen slows them down, so water, concrete and damp soil are what stop neutrons.

In the world every block on the straight line between the source and you shields: lead, iron, concrete and stone, brick, soil, wood, water. The pile's own graphite stack is a good shield. With a metre or a metre and a half of graphite between you and the core it is safe to stand beside the pile, but not over the open top.

## Dosimeter

The [DKS-04 dosimeter-radiometer](item:promptcritical:dosimeter_radiometer) in your hand shows dose rate and accumulated dose and clicks its counter. Natural background is about 0.1 µSv/h, which means rare clicks. The faster the clicks, the stronger the field. A continuous crackle means it is time to leave. Above 10 Sv/h the counter saturates.

## Radiation sickness

- **up to 0.25 Gy**: nothing noticeable
- **0.25–1 Gy**: blood changes
- **1–2 Gy**: grade I acute radiation sickness: nausea, weakness
- **2–4 Gy**: grade II: vomiting, hunger, bone marrow damage
- **4–6 Gy**: grade III: untreated, half of those exposed die
- **over 6 Gy**: grade IV: lethal
- **over 20 Gy**: cerebral form: death within minutes

Acute dose is what was taken recently. The body recovers, and the acute dose halves every 30 game days. The lifetime total stays on the dosimeter. Long-term effects, cancer and hereditary effects, are not modelled.

Spent fuel is not only waste. It still holds almost all its uranium (only part of the U-235 has burned), about one per cent plutonium and a few per cent of fission products, which are what make it so dangerous. A reprocessing plant separates all of that into three products: reprocessed uranium, plutonium and glass holding the high-level waste. That closes the nuclear fuel cycle.

## The line

Everything happens behind concrete and lead: nobody enters the canyon, work is done with manipulators. The [PUREX canyon](item:promptcritical:purex_cell) runs the apparatus within 8 blocks of it. Without a piece of apparatus, its stage stops:

- the [fuel shear](item:promptcritical:shearing_machine) chops the rods, the [dissolver](item:promptcritical:dissolver) dissolves them in boiling nitric acid;
- two [mixer-settler banks](item:promptcritical:mixer_settler) — two extraction cycles;
- the [oxalate precipitator](item:promptcritical:oxalate_precipitator) turns plutonium into PuO₂;
- the [denitration reactor](item:promptcritical:denitration_reactor) makes UO₃ from the uranium;
- the [vitrification melter](item:promptcritical:vitrification_melter) fuses the fission products into borosilicate glass.

Put fuel into the canyon, pipe in acid and kerosene, and put charcoal for the iodine filter into its slot. The reactor writes each assembly's composition into its fuel lot: burnup, remaining U-235, U-236 and plutonium. Without a lot, burnup is estimated from caesium-137.

## Extraction

Tributyl phosphate (30 % in kerosene) eagerly takes hexavalent uranium and tetravalent plutonium out of nitric acid and leaves almost all the fission products in the water. The distribution coefficient says how eagerly: about 20 for uranium at 3 M HNO₃, about 6 for plutonium, tenths for zirconium, thousandths for caesium and strontium. Eight counter-current stages extract nearly all the uranium and plutonium, and an acid scrub knocks out what impurities remain. Two cycles purify the product millions of times over; beyond that, purity is limited by aqueous droplets carried over with the organic.

Feed acidity on the panel is the main control. Too weak, and uranium and plutonium transfer poorly and are lost to the raffinate. Too strong, and zirconium follows them into the extract and dirties the product.

## Separating uranium from plutonium

Plutonium is reduced to the trivalent state (with uranous U(IV) or hydroxylamine). TBP does not take Pu(III), so the plutonium goes to the water and the uranium stays in the organic. With less reductant than plutonium, part of the plutonium goes with the uranium. The "Reductant" control sets the margin.

## Plutonium and criticality

Plutonium nitrate collects in a buffer tank before oxalate precipitation. The minimum critical mass of plutonium in solution with water is only 0.51 kg, and the safe mass is 0.23 kg; the red line on the buffer dial marks the safe mass. While the precipitator runs, the buffer holds grams. If it stops (no apparatus, store full), plutonium builds up, and above the critical mass comes the blue flash of a criticality excursion, with dose through the walls to everyone around. It happened at Mayak in the 1950s and 60s. PuO₂ comes out in 100 g lots, below the safe mass.

## Gases and waste

Krypton-85 is a noble gas: all of it goes up the stack, and the cloud drifts downwind. Iodine-131 is sparged into the off-gas during dissolution, and the charcoal filter holds back 99.9 % of it, as long as there is charcoal in the slot. Without charcoal, the iodine goes up the stack. The panel shows the public dose rate 500 m downwind.

Fission products from the first-cycle raffinate are vitrified: 15 % waste oxides in borosilicate glass, about 0.1 m³ of glass per tonne of fuel. The 50 kg canisters are hot and glowing, and they belong in the [glass vault](item:promptcritical:hlw_vault): a metre of concrete and ventilation.

Reprocessed uranium carries U-236, which absorbs neutrons. So reprocessed uranium is enriched a little higher than fresh uranium, and the cascade decides how much.

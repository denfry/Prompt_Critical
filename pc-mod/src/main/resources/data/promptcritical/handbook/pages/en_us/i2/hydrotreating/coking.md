## Delayed coking

Fuel oil from the bottom of the crude column is the heavy end nobody wants: thick, sulphurous, and worth less than the crude it came from. The [delayed coker](item:promptcritical:delayed_coker) cracks it hard. A tube furnace takes the feed to 495–505 °C, too fast for it to coke inside the tubes, and dumps it into a drum where it has time to finish: the light ends boil off, and what is left sets into solid [petroleum coke](item:promptcritical:petroleum_coke).

How much coke you get depends on the Conradson carbon of the feed (Gary and Handwerk): coke is 1.6 times the CCR, gas 7.8 + 0.144·CCR per cent, naphtha 11.3 + 0.34·CCR per cent, and the rest is coker gas oil. Our fuel oil has a CCR of 10 %, so a tonne gives 160 kg of coke, about 92 kg of gas, 147 kg of naphtha and 600 kg of gas oil.

There are two drums. One fills for 16 hours while the other is steamed, quenched with water and cut out with high-pressure jets; then they swap. The panel shows how full the active drum is and what the other one is doing. Coke drops into the output slots in a batch when the spent drum reaches hydraulic decoking. The plant runs 120 times faster than life, so a full drum cycle takes 14 minutes.

Run the furnace colder than 495 °C and the coke stays soft and wet with oil (amber lamp): more coke, less product. Hotter than 505 °C and the furnace tubes themselves start coking, twice as fast for every 10 K (red lamp).

Feed fuel oil by pipe to the sides. Naphtha leaves from the top and gas oil, sent on to the hydrotreater, from the bottom. About 30 % of the feed sulphur ends up as H₂S in the coker gas; a Claus unit touching the coker takes it. Petroleum coke burns in a furnace (a bit longer than coal) and can replace pitch coke as filler in the electrode extruder.

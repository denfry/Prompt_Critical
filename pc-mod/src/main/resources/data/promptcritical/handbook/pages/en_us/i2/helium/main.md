On Earth, helium is mined from natural gas. It is born underground in the alpha decay of uranium and thorium and collects in traps along with the gas. Usually there is little of it: Orenburg gas holds 0.055 %, half a cubic metre per thousand. But there is no other industrial source, and without it you cannot fill fuel rods, shield a weld or cool a superconductor.

## How it is separated

Helium boils at 4 K, before any other gas, so it is separated by cold: everything else is condensed and helium stays a vapour.

The [helium recovery unit](item:promptcritical:helium_recovery_unit) takes sales gas by pipe at the side. The first stage, at 4 MPa and −100 to −105 °C, liquefies methane and everything heavier. Nitrogen and almost all the helium stay in the vapour. Helium dissolved in the liquid is released by throttling into a stripping column and sent on as well. The second stage, at 95 K over boiling liquid nitrogen, liquefies the nitrogen and leaves a crude helium of 70–95 % He. The last step is adsorption on charcoal at 77 K, which strips the remaining nitrogen, methane and neon and gives 99.995 % helium.

Liquid nitrogen comes from the [air separation unit](item:promptcritical:air_separator). Without it the second stage cannot cool and there is no helium. Take helium from the top; the gas, minus its helium, goes back to the grid from the bottom. Recovery is about 93 %, as at real plants. The first-stage temperature on the panel is the main control. Warmer puts more methane in the vapour and uses more nitrogen; colder leaves more helium in the liquid.

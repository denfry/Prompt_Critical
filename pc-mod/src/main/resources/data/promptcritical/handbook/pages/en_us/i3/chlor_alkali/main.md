Chlorine, hydrogen and caustic soda are made from table salt and electricity. A current splits the brine: chlorine comes off the anode, hydrogen off the cathode, and caustic stays in solution. A tonne of chlorine costs about three megawatt-hours, so a chlorine plant is one of the biggest loads on the grid.

## Brine

The [brine treatment plant](item:promptcritical:brine_treater) dissolves salt to 310 g/l. Put salt bags in by hand or with a hopper, and pipe in water. Rock salt carries gypsum and magnesium chloride, which leaves about 300 mg/l of calcium and magnesium in raw brine. Calcium is settled out with soda ash, magnesium with caustic. The soda ash is made on site by bubbling boiler flue gas through cell liquor. If cell liquor or flue gas runs short, raw brine comes out of the bottom. The cells will take it, but the hardness settles in the pores of their diaphragms.

## Electrolysis

The [cell series](item:promptcritical:chlor_alkali_cell) is twenty diaphragm cells, the kind Soviet chlorine plants ran in the 1950s to 1970s. Membrane cells came later, and the mercury process gave pure caustic at the price of mercury in the effluent. The cell current comes from an [electrolysis rectifier](item:promptcritical:rectifier_unit) within 6 blocks, fed by a 6.3 kV cable at 96.5 % efficiency.

Cell voltage is the decomposition voltage (2.19 V at 90 °C), the chlorine overvoltage on the ruthenium oxide anode, the hydrogen overvoltage on the steel cathode and the ohmic drop. At 2 kA/m² that adds up to about 3.4 V. More current means more chlorine, but also more volts and more energy per tonne: 2.6 to 2.8 MWh of DC per tonne of chlorine. A cold cell runs worse; at full load its own current heats it to 90 °C.

Take chlorine from the top, hydrogen from the bottom and cell liquor (11 % NaOH, 15 % salt) from the sides. Current efficiency is about 96 %. It drops when the caustic gets stronger than normal, because OH⁻ ions cross the diaphragm to the anode and waste current on hypochlorite and oxygen.

## Hazards

Short brine lowers the anolyte level and strengthens the caustic. If the anolyte falls below the top of the diaphragm, hydrogen gets into the chlorine. Above 4 % the mixture explodes. The interlock trips the series, but the gas in the cells is already going off and throws chlorine out.

If the chlorine has nowhere to go (pipes blocked, tank full), header pressure rises and chlorine escapes through the seal pots. The "Cl₂ leak" lamp on the panel lights.

## Chlorine cloud

Chlorine is 2.5 times heavier than air and drifts with the wind. A Gaussian model works out the concentration. On a sunny day the cloud spreads out fast. On a clear, calm night it stays a dense tongue for hundreds of metres. You can smell it from 0.3 ppm. Nausea starts at 3 ppm, weakness and stinging at 10 ppm (IDLH), and above 50 ppm you choke. Harm builds up with dose: the chlorine probit weights concentration squared times time. A short breath far above the limit is worse than a long wait near it. Get out across the wind and uphill.

## Liquefaction

The [liquefaction unit](item:promptcritical:chlorine_liquefier) dries chlorine with sulfuric acid, compresses it to 0.6–1.0 MPa and condenses it with refrigeration. The colder it runs, the more chlorine turns liquid. But inerts and hydrogen stay in the tail gas, and their share rises. Above 4 % hydrogen in the tail gas the interlock stops the unit, which is why liquefaction depth is limited by hydrogen. Cell liquor absorbs the tail gas, making hypochlorite. Without cell liquor, the tail gas goes into the air as a cloud. Take liquid chlorine from the bottom. If storage is full, the relief valve dumps the overflow.

## Vinyl chloride and PVC

The [EDC unit](item:promptcritical:edc_unit) makes ethylene dichloride two ways. Direct chlorination runs ethylene with chlorine over ferric chloride at 50–70 °C. Oxychlorination runs ethylene, hydrogen chloride and oxygen over copper chloride at 220–250 °C. Above 260 °C the ethylene starts to burn.

The [EDC cracking furnace](item:promptcritical:edc_cracker) splits dichloroethane into vinyl chloride and hydrogen chloride at 480–530 °C. Conversion per pass stays around 55 %, because coke and by-products climb above that. Unconverted EDC goes back into the coil. Take vinyl chloride from the bottom and hydrogen chloride from the top, then pipe the HCl back to oxychlorination. That balances the route: all the chlorine from the cells ends up in vinyl chloride.

The [PVC autoclave](item:promptcritical:pvc_reactor) charges itself with 250 kg of vinyl chloride and water, starts the batch and runs it for about 6 game hours. Up to 70 % conversion the pressure equals the monomer's vapour pressure, and then it drops. The batch ends on that pressure drop. Leftover monomer is vented back to the tank, and the resin comes out in 25 kg bags. Temperature sets the grade: 50 °C gives K 70, 63 °C gives K 57. Vinyl chloride is a carcinogen, which is why the autoclave is closed and the resin is degassed.

## Hydrogen chloride and hydrochloric acid

The [HCl synthesis furnace](item:promptcritical:hcl_synthesis_furnace) burns chlorine in hydrogen on a graphite burner: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2 HCl, 2.53 MJ per kg of HCl. Pipe hydrogen, chlorine and water into the sides. Hydrogen goes in with a 5–10 % excess so that every bit of chlorine burns: below 5 % unburnt chlorine leaves with the tail gas into the air, and the alarm lamp lights. Use the furnace with an empty hand to light it on acid, switch it to dry gas and put it out; sneak-use to step the hydrogen excess.

On acid, the absorber above the burner takes the gas up in water as 33 % hydrochloric acid. On dry gas, the hydrogen chloride is cooled and dried and leaves as a gas for oxychlorination and trichlorosilane. Take either product from the bottom. Hydrogen chloride also comes back from EDC cracking: balancing the chlorine is up to you.

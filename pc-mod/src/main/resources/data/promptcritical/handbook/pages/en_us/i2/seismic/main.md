Oil and gas sit 0.6 to 2.5 km down, well below the bottom of the world. Digging for them gets you nowhere: you find them with seismic and reach them with a well. Each trap has a model in the deepslate below it, a dome of [sandstone](item:promptcritical:reservoir_sandstone) under [cap rock shale](item:promptcritical:cap_rock_shale), and shallow fields leak [oil seeps](item:promptcritical:oil_seep) at the surface.

## The trap

Oil is lighter than water and climbs up the bed until a dome of tight shale stops it. A filled dome is a field: gas or oil on top, water below the contact. One dome in five is empty because the seal cracked and everything leaked away.

## Shooting a survey

Plant [geophones](item:promptcritical:geophone) around the [seismic recorder](item:promptcritical:seismic_recorder), no further than 24 blocks out. You need at least four. Use a [seismic charge](item:promptcritical:seismic_charge) on the recorder to fire the shot hole. Ten seconds later it prints a [seismic section](item:promptcritical:seismic_section) of the nearest structure. More geophones cover a wider area: 64 blocks plus 16 for each one, up to sixteen.

## Reading the section

Seismic measures time, not depth. You see the crest as a two-way travel time, and the interpreter converts it with velocities known to about 3 %. Gas sometimes shows a bright spot, because gas sand reflects hard. Oil and water look the same.

The useful part is the pore pressure forecast by Eaton's method. Shale in an overpressured zone is undercompacted, so sound travels through it slower than the trend. The forecast is off by 5 to 10 %. If it reads heavier than your mud, weight up before you drill in.

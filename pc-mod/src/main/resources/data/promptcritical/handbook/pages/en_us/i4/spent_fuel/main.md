Fuel is more dangerous after the reactor than before it. Every fission produces two fragments, and nearly all of them are radioactive. The steel of the assembly turns radioactive under neutrons too: the cobalt in its impurities becomes cobalt-60.

## What glows in it

- **Caesium-137** (30 years): the main gamma source in cooled fuel, the 662 keV line.
- **Caesium-134** (2 years): made by neutron capture on stable caesium. The deeper the burnup, the more of it.
- **Strontium-90 and yttrium-90** (29 years): pure beta emitters, almost no gamma, but they make heat.
- **Iodine-131** (8 days): bright in the first weeks, almost gone after three months.
- **Zirconium-95 and niobium-95**: the niobium daughter lives longer, and after six months its activity is double the zirconium's.
- **Ruthenium-106** (1 year): its gamma comes through its short-lived daughter rhodium-106.
- **Cobalt-60** (5.3 years): from the steel of the assembly.

A VVER or PWR assembly at 40 MWd/kg holds about 2·10¹⁵ Bq of caesium-137 alone. After ten years of cooling, one metre from it unshielded you get tens of sieverts per hour, lethal in minutes. The fuel absorbs most of its own gamma, but what gets out is more than enough.

## Spent fuel pool

Discharged fuel stands under water for months and years on [pool racks](item:promptcritical:fuel_rack). The water does two jobs. It shields: three metres of water cut caesium gamma by hundreds of thousands, so you can stand over the pool. And it cools: a fresh assembly gives off tens of kilowatts of decay heat, and the water carries it away.

The rack takes any item with induced activity. Its panel shows decay heat, activity and the dose rate above the rack. Without water the rack heats up. Above 400 °C the amber lamp lights; above 800 °C the cladding oxidises and fails, the scenario of a drained pool fire. Keep the rack under water and do not carry irradiated fuel in your pocket: the dosimeter will tell you why.

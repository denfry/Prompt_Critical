## Rod ejection

A control cluster sits in a drive housing on the vessel head with 15.7 MPa below it. If the housing cracks, the pressure throws the cluster out of the core in a few hundredths of a second, and the hole it leaves is a small break of its own. The **Rod ejection** button on the VVER panel runs this as a drill. Press it again on a shut-down reactor to put the cluster back and seal the housing.

**How much reactivity.** It depends on where the control group was. With the group fully in at hot zero power, one cluster is about a third of the group's 2500 pcm, roughly 1.3β, because the flux piles up around the empty position. At power the group is nearly out and a cluster is worth about 0.2β. That is a fast but ordinary excursion, and the high-flux trip handles it.

**Above β.** Above prompt criticality the delayed neutrons cannot keep up, and the power runs away on prompt neutrons alone until the Doppler effect stops it. The Nordheim–Fuchs model gives the whole burst: energy E = 2(ρ₀ − β)/γ, with γ the Doppler coefficient over the fuel heat capacity, and a width of about 3.5Λ/(ρ₀ − β), a few milliseconds. Afterwards the core is as far below prompt critical as it had been above. The burst trips the reactor on high flux.

**What the fuel takes.** The damage measure is the fuel enthalpy at the hot spot: the start value (18 cal/g at 290 °C) plus four times the core average, since the flux is tilted towards the empty position. Cladding fails by pellet–clad mechanical interaction above max(60, 150 − 1.6·Bu) cal/g, so burnt fuel fails first. Fresh fuel fails at 170 cal/g on overheating. Above 230 cal/g the fuel fragments into the water, and the fragments cause a steam explosion in the core worth 0.1–2 % of their heat.

A 1.3β ejection on fresh fuel gives a few tens of cal/g and nothing fails. If one cluster carries the whole group's 2500 pcm, the ejection is several β and fragments the hottest fuel. That is why real plants limit what a single cluster is worth.

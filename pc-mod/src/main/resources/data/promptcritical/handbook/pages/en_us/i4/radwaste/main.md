A nuclear plant makes far more waste than spent fuel. Coolant cleanup resins catch cobalt-60 and caesium, filter cartridges clog with activated corrosion products, floor drains and decontamination solutions fill tanks. None of it can be poured away or burned. It has to be measured, classified, made solid and put somewhere it will stay put for as long as it matters: three hundred years for most of it, a million for some.

## Where it comes from

The [reactor water cleanup](item:promptcritical:reactor_water_cleanup) stands within 8 blocks of a power reactor and takes its thermal power. Per megawatt-year it catches about 10 GBq on its resin bed and filter, sends about 1.5 m³ of drain water out as liquid radioactive waste and wears out 3 litres of resin. Load fresh anion resin and [filter cartridges](item:promptcritical:filter_cartridge); spent ones come out at the bottom on their own, or when you press the button. Each failed fuel assembly adds as much caesium and iodine again.

PUREX sends its fission products to glass. Decontamination crews send their used solutions down the drain.

## Classification

Waste is sorted by specific activity, following the Russian OSPORB-99/2010 table. For solids, beta emitters up to 1 MBq/kg are very low level, up to 10 MBq/kg low level, up to 10 GBq/kg intermediate and above that high level. Alpha emitters and transuranics have limits ten and a hundred times lower. Waste that gives off more than about 2 kW per cubic metre is high level whatever its activity. Long-lived alpha above 400 Bq/g (IAEA GSG-1) cannot go near the surface.

The class decides the route. Classes 3 and 4 (low level and short-lived intermediate) go to a near-surface vault. Classes 1 and 2 (heat-generating, high level and long-lived) go deep.

Tc-99, I-129, C-14 and Ni-63 do not show up on a gamma spectrometer, and they decide what happens in ten thousand years. So they are not measured; they are scaled from caesium-137 and cobalt-60 with fixed ratios, which is how real plants do it.

## Evaporation and solidification

The [evaporator](item:promptcritical:lrw_evaporator) takes liquid waste and decontamination solutions by pipe, boils off about a cubic metre an hour and sends clean condensate out the top. The decontamination factor is ten thousand, so the condensate carries a ten-thousandth of the activity. Salts and activity stay in the sump until it reaches 300 g/l. Then the concentrate is solidified by whatever stands within 4 blocks:

- the [cementation mixer](item:promptcritical:cementation_mixer) needs [Portland cement](item:promptcritical:portland_cement) and empty [drums](item:promptcritical:waste_drum). A drum takes about 110 litres of concentrate at 10 % salts and comes out near 34 MPa. Above 20–25 % salts cement crumbles, and a drum below 4.9 MPa is rejected everywhere. Spent resin bags are cemented too, 35 % resin by volume;
- the [bitumenizer](item:promptcritical:bitumenizer) needs fuel oil by pipe and puts three times more concentrate into a drum (40 % salts). Bitumen burns, though, so it takes nothing high level;
- if the concentrate itself turned out high level, cement and bitumen are forbidden. Only the [vitrification melter](item:promptcritical:vitrification_melter) will take it, making aluminophosphate glass the way Mayak does.

The [supercompactor](item:promptcritical:waste_compactor) crushes filter cartridges and any contaminated junk. It fills a drum with 200 litres, presses it at 2000 tonnes (77 MPa on the drum bottom) into a puck six times smaller, and stacks pucks in an overpack drum.

## Glass and storage

Borosilicate glass holds up to 18.5 % waste oxides and must stay below its glass transition, 510 °C. Phosphate glass melts cooler but crystallises above 400 °C. The centre of a canister is hotter than its surface by q'''R²/4k. In the [vault](item:promptcritical:hlw_vault) the ventilation air warms by 30 K at design load. The ventilation lamp comes on if there is more heat than that or if any canister's centre runs too hot.

## Disposal

The [near-surface vault](item:promptcritical:near_surface_repository) accepts drums of classes 3 and 4 with surface dose rates up to 2 mSv/h. The [deep repository](item:promptcritical:deep_geological_repository) accepts everything solid, but glass only in a [copper canister](item:promptcritical:copper_canister) and only at up to 1.7 kW per package (the bentonite buffer must stay below 100 °C). Put packages in by hand or by hopper. A rejected package stays in the slot and the panel says why.

The panel also estimates the peak dose after closure. The containers hold for 300 years near the surface, 1000 years deep (100 000 in copper). After that the waste form leaches slowly: cement fastest, glass a million times slower. Groundwater carries each element at its own pace: caesium and plutonium stick to sand for tens of thousands of years, while iodine, technetium and carbon-14 travel almost as fast as the water. The river of the local catchment dilutes it all. Someone drinking that water and eating its fish should get less than 0.3 mSv a year. Near the surface the dose comes from the mobile long-lived nuclides; caesium-137 decays on the way. Deep down, even iodine needs millions of years.

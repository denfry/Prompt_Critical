Natural uranium holds only 0.711 % of fissile U-235. That is enough for a graphite pile, but a water reactor needs 3–5 %. The isotopes are chemically the same, so they are separated by mass: a ²³⁵UF₆ molecule is lighter than ²³⁸UF₆ by just 3 units in 352. A gas centrifuge throws the heavy molecules to the wall of a fast rotor and leaves the light ones nearer the axis.

## The centrifuge

A [gas centrifuge section](item:promptcritical:gas_centrifuge) is 20 machines in vacuum casings. Inside the rotor the gas circulates in countercurrent, down at the wall and up at the axis, so the radial separation adds up along the length, as in a column. Dirac gave the limit of separative power: δU ∝ Z·v⁴, where Z is the rotor length and v the peripheral speed. Double the speed and the power grows sixteenfold. A real machine reaches about half of the limit when its circulation is right.

Speed runs into strength. A thin rotor wall tears when the hoop stress ρv² reaches the material strength. Aluminium will not go past 420 m/s, maraging steel 500 m/s, carbon fibre about 700 m/s. The closer to burst speed, the more often rotors fail: at 80 % one machine in a hundred a year, at 95 % nearly half. A burst rotor wrecks its section and often the next one.

Pick the rotor design on the control panel with the hall stopped:

- **aluminium, 0.6 m** — early subcritical machines, about 1 SWU a year;
- **maraging steel, 1.5 m** — the long rotor runs above its first bending critical speed and fails five times more often (the Supercrit. lamp);
- **carbon fibre, 1 m** — short, fast, subcritical, 10–20 SWU a year.

## SWU

Separation is measured in separative work units (SWU), counted through the value function V(x) = (2x − 1)·ln(x/(1 − x)). One kilogram of 4.5 % uranium from natural feed with 0.25 % tails takes 6.9 SWU and 9.2 kg of natural uranium. Leaner tails save feed but cost more SWU. The best tails assay depends on prices: when uranium is cheap, tails are kept richer.

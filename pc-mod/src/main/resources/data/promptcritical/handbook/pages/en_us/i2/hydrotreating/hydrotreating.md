## Hydrotreating

Straight-run diesel from the [crude column](item:promptcritical:crude_column) carries about 1.2 % sulphur. Burnt as it is, every tonne puts 24 kg of SO₂ into the air. The [diesel hydrotreater](item:promptcritical:hydrotreater) strips it out with hydrogen over a cobalt–molybdenum catalyst at 6 MPa: mercaptans and thiophenes give up their sulphur as hydrogen sulphide, R–SH + H₂ → R–H + H₂S.

The reaction behaves as order 1.5 in sulphur, because the easy sulphides go first and the stubborn dibenzothiophenes hang on to the end. At 360 °C and a space velocity of 1.5 per hour, diesel leaves with about 50 ppm. Every 20 °C colder halves the rate constant and roughly doubles what is left. Above 420 °C in the bed hydrocracking starts; it releases heat, and the red lamp warns that the bed is running away.

Feed diesel and hydrogen by pipe to the sides or top; treated diesel leaves from the bottom. A straight-run feed eats about 0.3 % of its mass in hydrogen, roughly 27 litres of gas per litre of diesel. Hydrogen comes from the steam reformer.

Hydrogen sulphide leaves the catalyst with the recycle gas and is washed out by amine inside the unit. It has to go somewhere: a Claus unit must touch the hydrotreater. Without one, H₂S builds up in the recycle, smothers the catalyst (2 % H₂S halves its activity) and finally stops the feed. Load a fresh [catalyst](item:promptcritical:catalyst_hydrotreating) through the panel slot; coke slowly eats its activity over a few physical years.

Hydrogen also comes from the [catalytic reformer](item:promptcritical:catalytic_reformer), which is now buildable.

## Catalytic reforming

The reformer turns the gasoline cut of the crude column, a heavy naphtha with an octane number in the fifties, into reformate. Three adiabatic reactors with platinum–rhenium catalyst run at 2.5 MPa with six moles of recycle hydrogen per mole of feed. Naphthenes lose hydrogen and become aromatics, and aromatics are what the engine likes: RON follows the aromatic content of the liquid. The kinetics are Smith's lumps from 1959.

Dehydrogenation swallows heat. The first reactor loses 50–70 K across its bed, so a furnace reheats the stream before each reactor. The furnaces burn the unit's own hydrocracking gas first and top up from sales gas piped to the sides. Whatever own gas is left over can be drawn off the sides as fuel gas.

Pipe the gasoline and the sales gas to the sides. Reformate leaves from the bottom and hydrogen from the top, about 2 % of the feed mass, a couple of hundred litres of gas per litre of naphtha: more than a hydrotreater eats. Hotter inlet means more octane, more gas and faster coking. The panel shows RON, activity, the temperature drop in the first reactor and the coke.

Coke slowly smothers the platinum. When activity falls below half, stop the unit and regenerate it. With 100 mB of chlorine in the side tank the burn-off ends with oxychlorination and the platinum is redispersed; without chlorine the coke still burns, but the catalyst keeps only 85 % of what it had. Above 560 °C the platinum sinters for good. Fresh [Pt-Re catalyst](item:promptcritical:catalyst_reforming) goes into the panel slot.

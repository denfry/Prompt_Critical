## When the loop opens

A light-water reactor has two ways to lose its core: the water leaves, or the power that puts water back goes away. The VVER and the boiling water reactor both watch for each.

**A break.** Pull a coolant pump or the pressurizer off a loaded, pressurised plant and you have torn a pipe. A pump takes the main coolant pipe with it, a double-ended guillotine break; the pressurizer takes the surge line, about a sixteenth of that area. Put the block back and the break closes. The **LOCA** button on the reactor panel runs the same thing as a drill: first press opens a 1 cm² break in a cold leg, second press widens it to the guillotine, third isolates it.

What happens next is plain mechanics. Subcooled water at 15.7 MPa flashes to the hot-leg saturation pressure, about 11 MPa, in an instant. Then the two-phase mixture leaves through the break at the critical mass flux G = Cd·A·√(2ρΔp), and the pressure follows what is left in the vessel. Once the level drops below the break nozzle, only steam gets out, and the vessel vents like a gas tank: in seconds for the guillotine break, over tens of minutes for a small one. A boiling water reactor is already saturated, so a small break does not depressurise it at all. Decay heat keeps it at pressure until steam reaches the break or the ADS opens.

The reactor scrams the moment the accident layer takes over. Its panel then shows the accident's pressure, not the normal loop's, and the bottom line reads out the level, the peak cladding temperature, the hydrogen made and the containment pressure.

**The core.** While the fuel is under water, nucleate boiling holds the cladding a few degrees above saturation. Uncovered, it sits in steam and heats up on decay heat. Zirconium starts eating steam above about 900 K (Baker–Just), adding its own heat and 44 g of hydrogen per kilogram. Cladding balloons and bursts from about 800 °C; by the 1204 °C limit of 10 CFR 50.46 count the whole core as breached. Burst assemblies leak iodine into whatever water or steam is leaving.
